## In short

- **You can install CCTV to protect your own home.** That's lawful.
- **But it can't be positioned to intrude on your neighbour's privacy,** for example by watching their front door, windows or courtyard.
- **Courts balance it case by case:** where the camera points, what it records, why it's there, and what's done with the footage.
- **If a camera faces your home:** talk, then write, then approach the District Magistrate, a civil court, or the police if private moments are recorded or shared.

## What the courts have said

- **Allahabad High Court (2026).** In *Yogendra Maurya v. State of U.P.* (order dated 26 October 2026), the court said no private CCTV camera can be installed and positioned in a way that interferes with a neighbour's privacy. It directed the District Magistrate to decide the petitioner's complaint within a month.
- **Kerala High Court (2023).** Security can't be a pretext to snoop on neighbours. The court asked the State Police Chief to frame CCTV guidelines.
- **Calcutta High Court, upheld by the Supreme Court (2025).** Cameras inside a shared house need the consent of all occupants.
- **Kerala High Court (2025).** A camera protecting an elderly woman facing real threats was allowed to stay. Genuine safety needs count.

## Which law applies?

- **The Constitution.** Privacy is a fundamental right under Article 21 (*K.S. Puttaswamy*, 2017), and courts apply that principle in disputes between neighbours.
- **The DPDP Act doesn't cover a household camera.** Section 3(c)(i) excludes personal data processed for personal or domestic purposes. But cameras run by housing societies (RWAs), shops and offices are a different matter, and from May 2027 they will have duties under the Act.
- **Criminal law, for the serious cases.** Capturing or sharing images of a person's private area without consent is an offence under section 66E of the IT Act. Voyeurism and intruding on a woman's privacy are offences under sections 77 and 79 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita.
- **Civil law.** A civil court can order a camera to be moved, masked or removed.

## If a neighbour's camera faces your home

1. **Gather facts quietly.** Take photos showing the camera's position and angle relative to your door or windows. Note when it was installed.
2. **Talk first.** Many disputes end when the owner learns about privacy masking or simply turns the camera.
3. **Write a polite letter** asking them to reposition or mask the camera within a reasonable time. Keep a copy and proof of delivery.
4. **Approach the authorities if it continues:**
   - the **District Magistrate**, especially where it's a public-nuisance or law-and-order concern;
   - a **civil court**, for an order to move or remove the camera;
   - the **police**, if private acts are recorded, or footage is shared or used to harass.
5. **Your housing society** may help if the camera is in a common area.

**Don't damage, cover or remove the camera yourself.** That can expose you to a complaint.

## If you're installing a camera

- **Point it at your own property:** your gate, door and parking, not the neighbour's.
- **Use privacy masking.** Most cameras let you black out parts of the frame.
- **Skip audio.** Recording conversations is far more intrusive than video.
- **Secure the footage:** a strong password, two-factor login on the app, and no default passwords.
- **Auto-delete after a short period** unless there's an incident.
- **Never post other people's footage online** to shame or expose them.
- **In a shared house or building, get everyone's consent** for cameras inside common spaces.
- **Put up a small notice** saying CCTV is in use. It's courteous, and deters intruders.

## For housing societies, shops and offices

From 13 May 2027, most duties under the DPDP Act will apply to you as a data fiduciary: a clear notice that CCTV is in use and why, reasonable security for the footage, limited retention, access only for authorised people, and a way for residents or customers to raise concerns. It's sensible to set this up now.

## Common questions

**My neighbour says it's for security. Does that settle it?** No. Security is a legitimate reason, but the camera still has to be positioned so it doesn't intrude more than necessary.

**Can I ask to see what their camera records?** You can ask, and showing you the view often resolves the dispute. If they refuse, that can be something to raise with the District Magistrate or a court.

**Is a doorbell camera different?** It's judged the same way: by what it captures. A doorbell camera that only covers your own doorstep is very different from one that looks straight into a neighbour's flat.
