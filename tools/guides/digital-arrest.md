## In short

- **There is no such thing as a "digital arrest".** No police, CBI, ED, customs, RBI or court officer arrests anyone over a video call, keeps them "under watch" on camera, or asks for money to "verify" or "clear" their name.
- **Hang up.** Don't share details, don't install apps, don't transfer money.
- **Already paid? Call 1930 immediately.** The faster you report, the better the chance of freezing the money.
- **Tell your parents and grandparents.** Older people are the most frequent targets.

## How the scam works

A caller claims to be from the police, CBI, ED, customs, a courier company or TRAI. They say a parcel in your name had drugs, your Aadhaar was used for money laundering, or your number is linked to a crime. They move you to a video call, sometimes with someone in uniform and a fake police station or courtroom behind them. They may show "arrest warrants" or court orders with seals, which are forged. Then they tell you:

- you are under "digital arrest" and must stay on camera;
- you must not tell anyone, or you'll be arrested;
- to prove your innocence, you must transfer your savings to a "safe" or "RBI-verified" account, to be returned after "verification".

The money never comes back.

## Red flags

- Any official asking you to stay on a **video call** "under arrest"
- Any official asking you to **transfer money** to "verify" or "secure" it
- Pressure to keep it **secret**, even from family
- **Warrants, notices or court orders sent on WhatsApp**
- Calls from unknown numbers, sometimes with a +91 number that looks official
- Threats that get louder the more questions you ask

## If the call is happening now

1. **Disconnect.** You have no legal duty to stay on the call.
2. **Don't send money, OTPs, Aadhaar, PAN or bank details.** Don't install any app they ask for (screen-sharing apps let them see everything).
3. **Talk to someone you trust** immediately, a family member or friend. The secrecy is part of the scam.
4. **If you're genuinely worried**, call your local police station yourself, using a number you look up independently. Never use a number the caller gives you.

## If you haven't lost money: report the call

Report the number, WhatsApp account or message on **Chakshu**, the fraud-reporting facility on the Department of Telecommunications' Sanchar Saathi portal (sancharsaathi.gov.in, or the Sanchar Saathi app). This helps block the numbers so others aren't targeted.

## If you've already paid: the first hours matter

Do these in order, as fast as you can:

1. **Call 1930**, the national cyber fraud helpline. Give your phone number, the amount, your bank, the time of each transfer, and the receiving account or UPI ID.
2. **File a complaint on cybercrime.gov.in** under "Financial Fraud". Upload screenshots, call logs, transaction receipts and any fake documents. Note the complaint number.
3. **Tell your bank in writing**, by email and at the branch, asking it to block further transactions and to request a hold on the receiving accounts. Keep the acknowledgement.
4. **File an FIR** at your local police station or cyber police station, quoting your cybercrime.gov.in complaint number.
5. **Escalate if the bank doesn't help:** complain to the bank's grievance officer, and after 30 days, or if the reply is unsatisfactory, to the RBI Ombudsman at cms.rbi.org.in.

**Keep everything:** call logs, chat screenshots, the "documents" they sent, bank SMSes and statements. Don't delete the chats.

## Will the bank refund your money?

Until recently, often not. The RBI's 2017 rules limit your liability for **unauthorised** electronic transactions where the bank is at fault. But in a digital arrest, you make the transfer yourself, under fear, so banks have treated it as **authorised**, even when sudden, unusual transfers went through without a single check.

That is changing. In the Supreme Court's suo motu case on digital arrest scams, a bench led by the Chief Justice said on 29 October 2026 that the RBI's new rules must include a liability clause: banks must compensate victims where inadequate safeguards or a failure of due diligence contributed to the fraud, for not less than the amount lost. The Court also asked WhatsApp to propose measures against these scams. The RBI's new rules are expected by the end of 2026, and the case is next listed on 23 November 2026.

**What this means for you now:** in your complaint to the bank and the RBI Ombudsman, point out any warning signs the bank ignored, for example a large first-time transfer to a new payee, several transfers in a short time, or a transfer that emptied a long-held fixed deposit or savings account.

## Protect your family

- Tell older relatives, clearly: *no officer arrests anyone on a video call, and no officer asks for money.*
- Agree a family rule: anyone who gets such a call phones a family member before doing anything.
- Set lower daily transfer limits on bank and UPI accounts for elderly relatives, and turn on transaction alerts.
- Save **1930** in everyone's phone.

## Common questions

**What if they really are police?** Real police don't question or arrest people over video calls, and never ask for money transfers. If you're served a genuine notice, it comes through proper channels and you can verify it with the police station or court directly.

**I paid days ago. Is it too late?** Report anyway, immediately. Recovery is harder after a delay, but the complaint is still needed for the investigation and for any claim against the bank.

**Should I pay someone who promises to "recover" my money?** No. "Recovery agents" who contact victims are often part of the same fraud.
