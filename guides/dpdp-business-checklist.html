DPDP Act: what your business must do by 13 May 2027

A plain-language checklist for Indian businesses, startups, schools and apps on the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 and the DPDP Rules, 2025, with the key dates, duties and penalties.

In short

  • Most duties start on 13 May 2027. That's when notice, consent, security, breach reporting, retention and children's data rules all take effect.
  • They apply whether or not the Data Protection Board is ready. As of mid-November 2026, no Board Chairperson or Member has been appointed, but the deadline hasn't moved.
  • Penalties go up to ₹250 crore per breach, so this is a board-level issue, not just an IT task.
  • Start with a data map. You can't comply with rules about data you haven't found.

The timeline

Date What happens
13 November 2025 DPDP Rules notified. The Data Protection Board established in law.
13 November 2026 Consent manager registration opens (s. 6(9), Rule 4).
13 May 2027 Almost everything else: notices, consent, security safeguards, breach reporting, retention and erasure, children's data, Significant Data Fiduciary duties, and users' rights.

Does it apply to you?

Yes, if you process digital personal data in India: customer, employee, student, patient or user data held on computers, apps, websites or cloud services, or paper data that you digitise. It also applies to businesses outside India that offer goods or services to people in India.

Two important exclusions:

  • Personal or domestic use (s. 3(c)(i)), for example a family's own CCTV or phone contacts.
  • Data the person has made public themselves, or that is published under a legal duty (s. 3(c)(ii)).

In the Act's terms, you're a data fiduciary if you decide why and how data is processed. Vendors who process data for you are data processors, and you remain responsible for them.

Your checklist

1. Map your data (start now)

List every place you collect or hold personal data: website forms, apps, CRM, HR and payroll, CCTV, email marketing lists, WhatsApp groups, spreadsheets, and every vendor that receives data. For each, note what data, why you collect it, who can access it, where it's stored, how long you keep it, and whether it leaves India.

Flag children's data and data of persons with disability. They need extra steps (see 6 below).

2. Rewrite your privacy notice

Before or when you ask for consent, your notice must be clear and stand on its own. It should list, item by item, the personal data you collect and the specific purpose for each, and explain how people can withdraw consent, exercise their rights and complain to the Board. People must be able to read it in English or any language in the Eighth Schedule of the Constitution.

3. Fix consent, and make withdrawal easy

  • Consent must be free, specific, informed, unconditional and unambiguous, given by a clear action. No pre-ticked boxes.
  • Withdrawing consent must be as easy as giving it. If someone gives consent in one click, they should be able to withdraw it in one click.
  • Where you rely on a "legitimate use" instead of consent (for example, for employment purposes), document why.

4. Put security safeguards in place

Reasonable safeguards include encryption or masking of personal data, access controls, logs of who accessed what, and backups. Keep access logs and related data for at least one year so breaches can be detected and investigated. Write these duties into your vendor contracts too.

5. Prepare a breach playbook

When you become aware of a personal data breach, you must tell each affected person and the Data Protection Board without delay, and send the Board a detailed report within 72 hours (Rule 7). That's only possible if you've decided in advance:

  • who leads the response, and who can approve notifications;
  • how you'll reach affected users (email, SMS, in-app);
  • templates for the user notice and the Board report.

6. Children and persons with disability

  • Before processing a child's data (anyone under 18), you need verifiable consent from a parent or lawful guardian. The same applies to a person with disability who has a lawful guardian.
  • You must not track, monitor the behaviour of, or target ads at children.
  • Verifying age shouldn't mean collecting more data than you need. The Supreme Court has warned that checking everyone's age is itself a privacy risk, so choose methods that collect as little as possible.

This matters most for ed-tech, schools, coaching institutes, gaming, health and social apps.

7. Retention and erasure

Delete personal data once the purpose is served or consent is withdrawn, unless a law requires you to keep it. Large e-commerce, online gaming and social media platforms (above the user thresholds in the Third Schedule) must erase a user's data after three years of inactivity, after telling them at least 48 hours in advance.

8. Respond to users' rights

People can ask to access a summary of their data, correct it, complete it, erase it, and nominate someone to act for them. Publish contact details of a person who can answer questions about your data processing (a Data Protection Officer if you're a Significant Data Fiduciary), and set up a grievance process with a response timeline.

9. Significant Data Fiduciaries

If the government notifies you as a Significant Data Fiduciary (based on the volume and sensitivity of data you process), you'll also need a Data Protection Officer based in India, an independent data auditor, periodic Data Protection Impact Assessments, and a periodic data audit.

Penalties

The Schedule to the Act sets maximum penalties per instance:

Failure Up to
Reasonable security safeguards to prevent a breach ₹250 crore
Notifying a breach to the Board and affected users ₹200 crore
Children's data duties ₹200 crore
Significant Data Fiduciary duties ₹150 crore
Other breaches of the Act or Rules ₹50 crore

A six-month plan

Now to February 2027

  • Map your data and flag children's data
  • Add data-processing clauses to every vendor contract
  • Draft your new privacy notice and consent text

March to May 2027

  • Build one-click consent withdrawal
  • Put encryption, access controls and logging in place
  • Set up your 72-hour breach playbook and run a mock drill
  • Publish a contact person and a grievance process

Common questions

We're a small business. Does this apply to us? Yes. There's no general exemption for small businesses, though the government can notify exemptions for certain classes, such as startups. Check the Gazette before relying on one.

The Board has no members. Can we wait? No. The legal duties start on 13 May 2027 regardless, and High Courts have already been directing people to the Board. Waiting only leaves you exposed once it is staffed.

Do we need a consent manager? No. Consent managers are registered platforms people can use to manage consent across many businesses. You may need to work with them, but you don't have to become one.
