Your neighbour's CCTV camera faces your front door. Is that legal?

Ashwani KumarAdvocate · 3 November 2026 · 3 min read
View on LinkedIn

The Allahabad High Court has given a clear answer in principle. In Yogendra Maurya v. State of U.P. (order dated 26 October, reported 2 November), Justices Ajit Kumar and Garima Prashad said "no private CCTV camera can be installed and positioned in such a manner as to interfere with the privacy of a neighbour."

The petitioner said his neighbour's camera covered his home's entrance and exit. The court didn't decide whether this camera crossed the line; it directed the District Magistrate to decide his complaint within one month.

Other courts have drawn similar lines:
🔹 Kerala HC (2023): security can't be a pretext to snoop on neighbours; the court asked the State Police Chief to frame CCTV guidelines
🔹 Calcutta HC, upheld by the Supreme Court (2025): cameras inside a shared house need the consent of all occupants
🔹 Kerala HC (2025): a camera protecting an elderly woman facing real threats was allowed to stay

So it's a balance. Courts look at where the camera points, what it records, why it's there, and what's done with the footage.

Which law applies?
• DPDP Act: household CCTV is outside it, since s. 3(c)(i) excludes personal or domestic use. RWAs, shops and offices are a different matter
• Article 21: privacy is a fundamental right (Puttaswamy), and courts apply it between neighbours
• IT Act s. 66E: capturing or sharing images of a person's private area without consent is an offence
• BNS ss. 77 and 79: voyeurism, and intruding on a woman's privacy
• Civil courts can order a camera moved or removed

If a camera faces your home (slide 6): talk first, then write, gather photos of the angle, and approach the District Magistrate, a civil court, or police if private acts are recorded or shared. Don't damage the camera yourself.

If you're installing one (slide 7): point it at your own property, use privacy masking, skip audio, secure and auto-delete footage, never post others' footage online, and in a shared house, get everyone's consent.

My take: as cheap cameras spread, these disputes will multiply. Clear state guidelines on where private CCTV may point, as the Kerala HC urged, would save many neighbours a trip to court.
