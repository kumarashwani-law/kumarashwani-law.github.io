Can an AI company train on Indian news without permission?

Ashwani KumarAdvocate · 15 October 2026 · 3 min read
View on LinkedIn

In ANI Media Pvt. Ltd. v. Open AI OpCo LLC, CS(COMM) 1028/2024, the Delhi High Court has given India its first answer, at least for now.

On 24 July 2026, Justice Amit Bansal dismissed ANI's application for an interim injunction (I.A. 45300/2024). The key takeaways:

1️⃣ Jurisdiction (paras 48–55): Storing ANI's works on US servers is only the "terminal step in the chain of events" that begins with accessing them from India. Moving servers abroad cannot be used to evade Indian copyright law.

2️⃣ Storage is reproduction (paras 145–151): Under Section 14(a)(i), storing a work "in any medium by electronic means", temporarily or permanently, is reproduction. But Section 14 operates "subject to the provisions of this Act", so an act covered by Section 52 is not infringement.

3️⃣ Fair dealing under Section 52(1)(a)(i) (paras 190–255):
• Commercial use alone does not exclude the defence (para 190)
• "Private" is not limited to individuals and can cover a company using data in a closed space (paras 208–211)
• "Research" gets an updating construction and extends to machine learning (para 218)
• Instead of the US four-factor test, the Court applied a purpose test and a fairness test: no market substitution of ANI's news feed was shown, and the public interest in LLMs was established (paras 234–255)

4️⃣ Outputs (para 271): ChatGPT's outputs, including those generated using RAG, were prima facie not substantially similar to ANI's works, and ANI failed to show memorisation or regurgitation. The 14 articles relied on were published after the models' training cut-offs.

5️⃣ Balance of convenience (paras 262–270): ANI had not used the available opt-out to block OpenAI's crawlers, and an injunction would harm the public at large.

What it leaves open:
• Will this broad reading of "private use, including research" survive appeal or the final hearing? The Court itself notes its observations have no bearing on the outcome of the suit (para 274).
• How does it sit alongside GEMA v. OpenAI in Germany and Thomson Reuters v. Ross in the US Third Circuit, which went the other way on their facts?
• What does it mean for publishers' licensing strategies in India?

For publishers, AI developers and anyone advising on data licensing in India, this is now the reference point. The full judgment is attached.

I'd be interested to hear how others read the Section 52 analysis.
