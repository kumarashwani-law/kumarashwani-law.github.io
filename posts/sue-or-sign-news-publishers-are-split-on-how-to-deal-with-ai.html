Sue or sign? News publishers are split on how to deal with AI companies.

Ashwani KumarAdvocate · 23 October 2026 · 3 min read
View on LinkedIn

Suing:
🔹 Canadian publishers v. Anthropic: Postmedia, Torstar, The Globe and Mail and The Canadian Press filed suit in Ontario on 6 October, alleging copyright infringement, unjust enrichment and breach of their websites' terms in building Claude
🔹 USA Today Co. v. OpenAI: filed in October, seeking $250 million. It alleges paywalled articles were used for training and that ChatGPT's summaries cut traffic
🔹 ANI v. OpenAI: the Delhi High Court refused an interim injunction in July, finding training likely fair dealing for "research". ANI has appealed

Signing:
🔹 On 8 September, the Times Group (Times of India, Economic Times) and the Indian Express Group each partnered with OpenAI. ChatGPT will show summaries of their reporting with attribution and links back. The Indian Express had sought to join ANI's case in 2025.

What the Indian deals don't disclose: whether content can be used for training, how much is paid, or for how long.

My take: until the law settles, the licence is the law between a publisher and an AI company. If you're drafting one, these 10 clauses matter (slides 5–6):
1️⃣ Scope of use: training, real-time retrieval and display are separate rights
2️⃣ Excerpt limits: maximum length, no full-text reproduction, paywalls respected
3️⃣ Attribution and working links
4️⃣ Misattribution fixes: fast correction when AI invents stories in your name
5️⃣ Payment model, usage reports and audit rights
6️⃣ Exit and deletion, including content already used in trained models
7️⃣ Release of past claims: does signing waive claims for past scraping?
8️⃣ Exclusivity and most-favoured-nation terms
9️⃣ Personal data in archives and DPDP duties
🔟 Indian law and an Indian forum for disputes

The India gap: our Copyright Act has no specific text-and-data-mining exception, so courts stretch "fair dealing". The EU is consulting on opt-out registers and fair compensation (comments close 3 November), and MeitY's AI consultation paper is due around November. Smaller publishers without bargaining power risk getting neither a deal nor a day in court.
