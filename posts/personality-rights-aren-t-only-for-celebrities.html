Personality rights aren't only for celebrities

Ashwani KumarAdvocate · 18 November 2026
View on LinkedIn

On 16 November, the Madras High Court restrained Blinkit from using the persona of Saravanan Hari, the Chennai Super Kings superfan, without his written consent.

📰 The facts
• Since around 2013, Hari has appeared at CSK matches with a yellow-painted face, curly wig, moustache, Dhoni's No. 7 and a thumbs-up pose. It is widely covered on TV.
• On 23 March 2025, a Blinkit ad on the front page of the Sunday Times showed a cartoon figure with the same look and pose.
• People he knew asked whether he was endorsing Blinkit. Pre-suit mediation failed, and he sued, claiming ₹1.1 crore.

⚖️ Blinkit's defence: the characters were fictional and didn't resemble him.
The court found a clear resemblance: the same pose was reproduced, and the public was likely to be misled into thinking it was him.

💬 The key line (Justice P.B. Balaji):
"The constitution protects the rights of every individual and it need not be restricted or limited to celebrities like actors and sportspersons."
Blinkit could not exploit his personality rights, "whatever little it may be", for commercial gain without consent.

📌 Relief (interim): no commercial use without written consent; the ad's artwork and emails must be preserved. The publisher's liability will be decided at trial. Next hearing: 14 December.

🧭 Where this fits
In T. Rangaraj (January 2026), the Madras High Court refused takedowns where posts merely circulated, insisting on commercial exploitation. Hari's case applies the same test to a non-celebrity. Read together, the test is identifiability plus commercial use without consent, not fame.

🔐 The privacy-law gap
Why not the DPDP Act? Section 3(c)(ii) excludes personal data that the person made publicly available, and a fan appearing on TV at matches may fall within that exclusion. Most of the Act also only applies from 13 May 2027. Personality rights and passing off fill that gap.

✅ For brands and agencies
1️⃣ Get written consent for any recognisable person, even as a cartoon or look-alike.
2️⃣ Don't recreate a known pose or signature look.
3️⃣ AI-generated look-alikes carry the same risk.

My take: if people recognise you, a brand needs your permission. That protects ordinary people without turning the right into a tool to silence fans, critics or the news.
