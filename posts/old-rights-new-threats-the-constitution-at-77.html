Old rights, new threats: the Constitution at 77

Ashwani KumarAdvocate · 26 November 2026 · 3 min read
View on LinkedIn

On the eve of Constitution Day, Chief Justice of India Surya Kant said something every lawyer should take seriously:

"Today, we are dealing with artificial intelligence, technological challenges, and global issues. Geographical boundaries have become increasingly irrelevant."

He called digital arrest scams "digital dacoities", said courts must meet them with innovative technological solutions, and asked whether the judiciary is equipped for AI. He also stressed that justice must stay timely and affordable.

Our fundamental rights were written for people facing power. In 2026, that power is often a scam network, a content filter or an algorithm. Here is where those rights are being tested this year:

→ Article 21, life and dignity: digital arrest scams. On 29 October, the Supreme Court said the RBI's new rules must make banks compensate victims, at least the amount lost, where a failure of due diligence enabled the fraud.

→ Article 19(1)(a), free speech: LiveLaw says its Instagram reels on Supreme Court hearings were restricted within about 15 minutes, with no order, notice or reasons. Its challenge to automated filtering under Rule 4(4) of the IT Rules is before the Delhi High Court.

→ Article 21, privacy: the Supreme Court has issued notice on a petition questioning police use of facial recognition at protest sites without a law authorising it. Puttaswamy requires a law, a legitimate aim and proportionate means.

→ Article 14, fair process: the Telangana High Court suspended a Collector's land order said to have been passed with AI tools without verifying facts. The Supreme Court's draft rules propose a complete ban on AI deciding cases.

The CJI also pointed to Articles 32 and 226, the writ powers of the Supreme Court and High Courts, as the guardians of these rights. In most tech cases, though, the route starts lower down: 1930 and your bank for fraud, the platform's grievance officer and the Grievance Appellate Committee for blocked content, and the High Court when the State acts without law or reasons.

My view: the Constitution doesn't need rewriting for AI. It needs applying, quickly and affordably, to harms that move faster than our procedures.
