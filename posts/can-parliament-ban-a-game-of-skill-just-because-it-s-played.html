Can Parliament ban a game of skill just because it's played for money? The Supreme Court will decide.

Ashwani KumarAdvocate · 5 November 2026
View on LinkedIn

India's Promotion and Regulation of Online Gaming Act, 2025 bans all online money games, whether of chance, skill or both. With its Rules, it took full effect on 1 May 2026:
🔹 Offering or facilitating money games: up to 3 years and/or ₹1 crore fine (3–5 years and up to ₹2 crore for repeat offences)
🔹 Advertising them: up to 2 years and/or ₹50 lakh
🔹 Banks and payment systems can't process them; sites can be blocked under the IT Act
🔹 E-sports and online social games are allowed, and e-sports will be registered
🔹 A new Online Gaming Authority decides what counts as a money game: stakes, expected winnings, revenue model, and whether rewards can be cashed out

The government says around 45 crore people were affected by money gaming, with losses above ₹20,000 crore.

The challenge (Head Digital Works v. Union of India and connected cases): the Supreme Court moved the High Court cases to itself in 2025. On 5 August 2026, a bench led by CJI Surya Kant agreed to a final hearing and asked parties to complete pleadings. There's no interim stay, so the law applies.

Three questions for the Court:
1️⃣ Competence: is this "betting and gambling", a State subject, or within Parliament's powers?
2️⃣ Article 19(1)(g): since Chamarbaugwala (1957) and K.R. Lakshmanan (1996), games of substantial skill have been treated as legitimate business. Can Parliament still ban them?
3️⃣ Proportionality: is a total ban justified when KYC, deposit limits and age checks exist?

The twist: in DGGI v. Gameskraft (27 May 2026), a two-judge bench held that real-money games are "betting and gambling" for GST, whether skill or chance, and upheld 28% GST on full deposits, including about ₹21,000 crore against Gameskraft. If skill doesn't matter for tax, will it matter for the Constitution?

My take: the harms are real. But the test is whether a total ban is the least restrictive answer, and whether users will simply move to offshore betting apps that pay no tax and protect no one.

Until the Court rules (slide 7): no stake-based games, even of skill; influencers and media face jail terms for ads; e-sports should watch for registration notices; and free-to-play games with cash-out rewards may count as money games.
