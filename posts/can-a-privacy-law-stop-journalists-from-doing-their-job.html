Can a privacy law stop journalists from doing their job? India's biggest media houses have asked the Supreme Court.

Ashwani KumarAdvocate · 26 October 2026 · 3 min read
View on LinkedIn

Reuters reported on 26 October that 21 news publishers, acting through the Digital News Publishers Association (DNPA), have challenged the Digital Personal Data Protection Act. DNPA members include Network18, NDTV, the Indian Express and Times Internet. The case is listed for hearing on 27 October. They want key provisions struck down before they take effect in May 2027.

Their four concerns:
🔹 Notice and consent (ss. 5–6): journalists may need consent from the very people they investigate
🔹 Erasure (ss. 8(7), 12): withdrawal of consent and erasure with no public-interest safeguard could force stories off the record
🔹 Information demands (s. 36 with Rule 23): the Centre can call for information from any data fiduciary, risking exposure of sources
🔹 Blocking (s. 37): access can be blocked "in the interests of the general public"

Underneath it all: there is no exemption for journalism. The 2018 draft, the 2019 Bill and the 2021 JPC version all had one. It was dropped in 2022. The State gets wide exemptions under s. 17; the press gets none.

The government's position: in July 2025, MeitY told media bodies no exemption was needed, said journalists aren't data fiduciaries, and offered FAQs. Separate petitions by Venkatesh Nayak, The Reporters' Collective and NCPRI (on the RTI amendment and s. 36) were referred to a larger bench in February 2026, without a stay.

Today, a journalist's only clear relief is s. 3(c)(ii): data the person (or a legal duty) made public falls outside the Act. Most investigative material doesn't fit.

My take: fix it, don't scrap it.
1️⃣ Put the exemption in the law; an FAQ can't override the text
2️⃣ Base it on purpose (journalism in the public interest), not status, so freelancers are covered
3️⃣ Keep security and breach duties; newsrooms hold sensitive data too
4️⃣ Require independent or judicial approval before s. 36 demands reach journalistic material
5️⃣ Apply a public-interest test before published reports or archives are erased

The EU, UK and Singapore all build this balance in (slide 5). Newsroom checklist on slide 7: subscriber and employee data is covered whatever the court decides.
