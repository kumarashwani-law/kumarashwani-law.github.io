Blocked in 15 minutes. No order. No reasons.

Ashwani KumarAdvocate · 25 November 2026 · 3 min read
View on LinkedIn

That is what LiveLaw says happened to its Instagram reels reporting Supreme Court hearings. A reel on the Election Commission's affidavit on the electoral roll revision was restricted in India within about 15 minutes of posting. Two more court reports followed in the next three days. Another was taken down the night before LiveLaw's case was heard.

LiveLaw says it received no blocking order, no notice and no reasons. Meta said the content "went against local law".

On 19 November, the Delhi High Court issued notice on LiveLaw's challenge to Rule 4(4) of the IT Rules, 2021.

What the rule says:
→ 2021: large platforms had to "endeavour" to use automated tools.
→ February 2026 amendment: they "shall deploy" automated tools against rape and child sexual abuse material, and against content "exactly identical" to content already removed.
→ The same amendment cut the takedown window from 36 hours to 3.
→ Under Rule 7, a platform that fails to comply can lose its safe harbour. For a platform, over-blocking is the safe option.

The numbers are striking. Meta restricted more than 5.4 million items in India in January–June 2026, against a little over 41,000 in the previous six months.

What is missing, according to the petition:
1. No notice to the publisher. Rule 4(4) only requires a notice to users trying to view the content.
2. Human review is mentioned, but nothing follows if it is skipped.
3. No order or request is shown to the person whose content is blocked.
4. The grievance route takes weeks. A news report is stale in hours.

LiveLaw asks the Court to strike down or read down the "exactly identical" requirement so it excludes news and court reporting, quash two unpublished MeitY advisories of 8 and 10 October 2026, and direct disclosure of the "legal requests" behind the restrictions.

The Union and Meta have four weeks to reply. The next hearing is on 18 January 2027.

My view: automated matching makes sense for child sexual abuse material. But when the same machinery restricts journalism and court reporting, it decides what citizens can know without telling anyone why. In Shreya Singhal, the Supreme Court upheld blocking under Section 69A because it came with reasons and a hearing. The question now is whether a mandatory filter can skip both.

If your own post or page is restricted, ask the platform in writing whether it acted on a government request or on its own systems, and keep screenshots of every notice.

Should a machine ever restrict a news report before a person has looked at it?

Views are personal and for general information only; not legal advice. The petition's claims are yet to be answered by the Union and Meta.
