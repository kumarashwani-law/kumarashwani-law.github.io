An AI detector's score is not proof

Ashwani KumarAdvocate · 23 November 2026 · 3 min read
View on LinkedIn

Speaking in Hyderabad on 21 November at the Commonwealth Legal Education Association's conference, CJI Surya Kant said:

"No student should therefore be held guilty of misconduct on the word of a machine alone."

His concern: where many students write in English as a second or third language, an honest student could be "branded a cheat on the strength of a number that no one can explain."

📊 The evidence
The study he cited (Patterns, 2023) tested seven widely used AI detectors:
• Essays by native English speakers: under 2% wrongly flagged
• Essays by non-native speakers: over 61% wrongly flagged
Simpler vocabulary reads as "machine-like". In India, that bias falls on most students.

🎓 Already happening
• 2024: a Jindal LLM student was failed after a detector rated his answers 88% AI-generated. He denied using AI and said there were no AI rules. The Punjab & Haryana High Court issued notice.
• November 2026: law students in Nepal filed a writ against penalties based solely on detection software.

⚖️ The legal lens
A misconduct finding needs a specific charge, evidence the student can see and answer, a hearing, and a reasoned decision.
A detector gives a probability, no reasons, and no way to cross-examine the tool. A penalty resting only on that score is open to challenge as based on no real evidence.

🔐 The data protection gap
GDPR (Art. 22) gives people a right not to be subject to decisions based solely on automated processing, and a right to human review. India's DPDP Act has no equivalent. Students, job applicants and borrowers must rely on natural justice instead.

✅ What the CJI proposed
1️⃣ No blanket ban on AI; it is "neither practicable nor wise".
2️⃣ Clear rules in advance: what's allowed, what must be disclosed, what counts as outsourcing the thinking.
3️⃣ Better assessment: oral explanations, discussing authorities, spotting errors in AI answers.
4️⃣ Model AI-disclosure guidelines for law schools and journals.

My take: treat a detector flag as a tip, not a verdict. A flag can justify a conversation, asking the student to explain their work and sources. It can't justify a penalty by itself. Universities, employers and journals should write that rule down before the first student is accused.
