A farmer's land dispute. An order he says he was never heard on. And, allegedly, an AI tool doing the drafting.

Ashwani KumarAdvocate · 10 November 2026 · 3 min read
View on LinkedIn

On 3 November, Justice N.V. Shravan Kumar of the Telangana High Court suspended the Jangaon District Collector's order of 8 September in a land dispute. The farmer says it was passed ex parte, without examining land records, and generated with AI tools; reportedly, staff photographed grievance applications and fed them into AI tools.

What the court did:
🔹 Took "serious exception" to relying on AI-generated material without verifying facts
🔹 Noted that negligence over property rights can have severe consequences for farmers and ordinary citizens
🔹 Questioned how a Collector could act without hearing the parties, relying only on automation tools
🔹 Issued notice to the Revenue Principal Secretary, the Collector and the Tahsildar, and asked the Advocate-General what action is proposed against officials

The next hearing is 18 November. These are allegations the authorities are yet to answer.

It isn't a one-off. In September, the Supreme Court set aside a ₹425 crore customs penalty built on non-existent cases, saying AI "may well serve as training wheels" but not the pilot's seat. This month, the Bombay High Court quashed a Mumbai customs order citing two untraceable Supreme Court judgments.

Why an AI-made order can't stand:
✔ Natural justice: the affected person must be heard
✔ Application of mind: a power given to the Collector can't be handed to a tool
✔ Reasoned orders: invented facts or cases aren't reasons
✔ Articles 14 and 300A: no arbitrary action, no loss of property except by law

There's a privacy problem too. A grievance application carries names, phone numbers, survey numbers and ID documents. Uploading it to a public AI tool may place citizens' data on a private company's servers. Under the DPDP Act the State can be a data fiduciary, and most of its duties apply from May 2027.

My take: AI can help draft, but only the officer can decide. Revenue offices need a written AI policy (verify, disclose, sign, and no citizens' data in public chatbots), and quashing orders isn't enough without accountability.
