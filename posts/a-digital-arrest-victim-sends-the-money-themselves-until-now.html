A "digital arrest" victim sends the money themselves. Until now, that meant the bank could say: not our problem. The Supreme Court has just changed that conversation.

Ashwani KumarAdvocate · 1 November 2026
View on LinkedIn

On 29 October, a bench of CJI Surya Kant, Justice Joymalya Bagchi and Justice V. Mohana, hearing the suo motu case on digital arrest scams, told the RBI that its new rules must include a liability clause:
🔹 Banks must compensate victims where inadequate safeguards or a failure of due diligence contributed to the fraud
🔹 Compensation of not less than the amount the customer lost
🔹 The bank pays, not just the employee involved ("The bank will have to pay," said the CJI)
🔹 Safeguards to consider: temporarily holding transactions out of line with an account's history, automatic alerts to police, and verification before release

Why this matters:
The RBI's 2017 circular gives zero liability for "unauthorised" transactions where the bank is at fault. But in a digital arrest, the victim authorises the transfer, under fear and deception. So victims have often fallen outside it, even when the bank let sudden, unusual transfers through without a single check.

What's next: the RBI has proposed notifying its KYC Amendment Directions by 30 November (also the next hearing) and bringing them into force by 31 December. Separately, a draft would let banks flag and hold suspected mule-account transactions, and the government has proposed a one-hour delay on payments of ₹10,000 or more.

My take: five questions the final rules must answer.
1️⃣ Who decides that due diligence failed? Not the bank alone; the burden should stay on the bank, as in 2017
2️⃣ What counts as due diligence? Clear triggers: large first-time transfers, new payees, elderly or dormant accounts
3️⃣ How fast is the refund? A provisional credit within days, not years of litigation
4️⃣ Who is covered? NBFCs, wallets and payment apps too
5️⃣ What about genuine payments? Holds need short time limits and a quick release route

If you get the call: no Indian agency arrests anyone over a video call, and no genuine officer asks you to transfer money to "verify" it. If you've already paid, call 1930 or report at cybercrime.gov.in immediately, tell your bank in writing, file an FIR, and escalate to the RBI Ombudsman if needed (slide 7).

Please share this with your parents and grandparents. They're the most frequent targets.
