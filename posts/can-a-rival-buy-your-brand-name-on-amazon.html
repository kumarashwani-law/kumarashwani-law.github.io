Can a rival buy your brand name on Amazon?

Ashwani KumarAdvocate · 19 November 2026

Kent RO has sued Amazon in the Delhi High Court (CS(COMM) 1145/2026). It alleges that its well-known mark "KENT" is sold as an advertising keyword, so shoppers searching for Kent purifiers see competing products first.

🔎 How keyword ads work
A seller bids on "KENT". A shopper searches "KENT". A rival's product appears at the top, marked "Sponsored". The platform earns on every click. The word "KENT" may never appear in the ad, which courts call "invisible use".

📅 Where the case stands
• Justice Tejas Karia issued summons on 16 October; Amazon has accepted notice.
• Kent placed a video on record showing the keyword bidding process.
• No interim relief yet. The application will be heard on 8 December.

⚖️ The law the court will apply
1️⃣ Google v. DRS Logistics (Division Bench, 2023): using a mark as a keyword is "use" in advertising even if it's never shown, but it isn't infringement per se. There must be confusion, unfair advantage or dilution. Google got no safe harbour because it actively promoted trademarks as keywords.
2️⃣ Hindware v. Grohe (May 2026): invisible keyword use is use; Google "was not neutral but an active commercial participant". Google has appealed.

❓ Three questions for a marketplace
• Who is using the mark: the seller who bids, or the platform that runs the auction and earns from it?
• Is there confusion? The shopper is already inside the store and the ad says "Sponsored", but KENT is a well-known mark, which gets wider protection.
• Is Amazon a passive intermediary? After DRS and Hindware, the search engine that sold keywords didn't get safe harbour. Amazon will have to show its role is different.

✅ What this means now
• Brands: search your own name on marketplaces and keep a record of what appears.
• Sellers: bidding on a rival's well-known mark is a real litigation risk; generic terms are safer.
• Platforms: "we're just an intermediary" is harder to argue.

My take: keyword ads aren't wrong in themselves. They're lawful unless they confuse buyers or take unfair advantage of a mark's reputation. For a well-known mark like KENT, on a platform that profits from every click, that line is easier to cross.
