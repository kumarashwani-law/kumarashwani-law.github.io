Can a privacy law quietly weaken the Right to Information? The Supreme Court hears that question again on 17 November.

Ashwani KumarAdvocate · 12 November 2026 · 3 min read
View on LinkedIn

Last month I wrote about the news publishers' challenge to the DPDP Act. But the larger case, Venkatesh Nayak v. Union of India, goes well beyond journalism. RTI activist Venkatesh Nayak, NCPRI, The Reporters' Collective and Nitin Sethi filed in February. The Court issued notice, refused an interim stay and referred the matter to a larger bench. The publishers' plea (DNPA) joined in October.

The central challenge is s. 44(3), which rewrote RTI s. 8(1)(j):
🔹 Before: personal information was exempt only if unrelated to public activity or an unwarranted invasion of privacy, and could be disclosed if a larger public interest justified it
🔹 After: "information which relates to personal information" is exempt, full stop
🔹 The clause's own public-interest test is gone, and so is the proviso that what can't be denied to Parliament can't be denied to a citizen

Why RTI users worry: officials' assets and qualifications, welfare beneficiary lists used in social audits, contractor payments and recruitment records can all now be refused as "personal information".

Five more provisions are under challenge:
1️⃣ s. 17: wide exemptions for State agencies
2️⃣ s. 36 with Rule 23(2): the Centre can demand data from any fiduciary, and the person may not be told
3️⃣ s. 18 with Rule 17: Board members chosen by an executive-led committee
4️⃣ s. 33(1): penalties for a "significant" breach, a term the Act doesn't define
5️⃣ No journalism exemption (the publishers' plea)

Both sides have a case. Petitioners call it a "sledgehammer" where a "chisel" was needed, relying on Subhash Chandra Agarwal (2019). Defenders point to Puttaswamy, to RTI misuse in private disputes, and to s. 8(2), which still allows disclosure where public interest outweighs harm. The CJI said "some ironing out of the creases" might be needed.

My take: privacy and transparency aren't opposites. Restore a public-interest test inside s. 8(1)(j), at least for public functionaries, and bring back the Parliament proviso. Put independent oversight on s. 36 demands, and make the Board truly independent.

What to watch on 17 November: the Union's reply, whether a larger bench is constituted, and a timeline before May 2027, when most of the Act's duties begin.
