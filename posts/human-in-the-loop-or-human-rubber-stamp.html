Human in the loop, or human rubber stamp?

Ashwani KumarAdvocate · 24 November 2026 · 3 min read
View on LinkedIn

Last week, Lord Sales, Deputy President of the UK Supreme Court, said something most judges avoid saying out loud: AI could one day decide some court cases.

"For low-value civil disputes, we may eventually accept forms of automated determination, particularly where parties agree."

His reason is practical. Courts are facing a "tsunami" of claims written and filed with AI, and he thinks courts may have to use AI to cope.

But the more important part of his speech was a warning:

"The 'human in the loop' can gradually become the human rubber stamp."

He calls it automation bias: "the tendency to accept the machine's answer precisely because the machine usually gets things right." The long-term danger, he said, is that people "gradually lose the ability to know whether it has got the answer wrong."

He also asked a question every law firm should answer. If AI does the research and first drafts that juniors used to do, "Where does the experienced senior lawyer come from?" In his words, the slower human process was producing "today's legal work and tomorrow's lawyer."

India has drawn a harder line:
→ July 2025: the Kerala High Court's AI policy bars district courts from using AI to arrive at findings, reliefs, orders or judgments.
→ June 2026: the Supreme Court's AI Committee published draft Regulations for Use of AI in Courts, 2026, proposing a complete ban on AI deciding judicial outcomes, built on "human primacy". Lawyers would have to disclose AI-assisted filings.
→ The CJI has said AI can help with research and managing work, but judgment must stay with judges.

I think India is right to keep AI out of deciding cases for now. But a ban on AI verdicts does not solve the quieter problem Lord Sales describes. That problem sits with us: lawyers who accept a polished draft because it is usually right.

For young lawyers like me, a few habits help:
1. Do some work the slow way. Draft a few pleadings and research notes yourself.
2. Read AI output like the other side's draft. Look for what is wrong, missing or invented.
3. Verify every citation and quotation against the official report.
4. Expect to disclose AI use. The draft rules would require it.

Speed is useful. Judgment is the job.

Would you accept an AI decision in a small claim if both sides agreed? I'd like to hear from lawyers and non-lawyers.
