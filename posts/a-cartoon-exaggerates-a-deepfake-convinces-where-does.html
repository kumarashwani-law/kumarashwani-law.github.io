A cartoon exaggerates. A deepfake convinces. Where does political satire end?

Ashwani KumarAdvocate · 7 November 2026
View on LinkedIn

The Delhi High Court gave an early answer on 6 November in Rekha Gupta v. Aam Aadmi Party & Ors. (Justice Manoj Jain). The Delhi Chief Minister sued over AI-generated videos and images posted from the party's verified accounts, allegedly showing her as a liquor vendor serving drinks and as head of a "Ghar Ghar Theka" scheme. The court described the visuals as a "near-perfect imitation."

What the court said:
🔹 Public figures must accept criticism and political humour, and parties remain free to attack government policies
🔹 But there is a "thin line": satire "cannot be permitted to create irreparable wound on someone's dignity"
🔹 AI makes realistic fakes easy, but responsibility for making and posting them stays with humans
🔹 Content that spreads fast on social media risks irreparable harm

It ordered six URLs taken down immediately and listed the case for 4 December. This is an interim order; the defendants' case is yet to be heard.

Why AI changes the satire question: satire works because the audience knows it isn't real. A cartoon or mimicry is obviously exaggeration. A realistic AI video can pass as real, and once shared without context, the joke disappears and only the false impression remains.

The legal frame: Article 19(1)(a) protects satire; Article 19(2) permits restrictions for defamation. Civil suits, criminal defamation (BNS s. 356), the February 2026 IT Rules on labelling synthetic content, and Election Commission advisories on deepfakes all apply.

My take, a five-question test:
1️⃣ Would a reasonable viewer know it isn't real?
2️⃣ Is it clearly labelled as AI-generated or parody, on the content itself?
3️⃣ Does it target a policy, or fabricate conduct by a person?
4️⃣ Who posted it? An official party account carries more responsibility
5️⃣ Would it be acceptable as a cartoon? If only the realism makes it hurt, the realism is the problem

For every party, campaigner and creator (slide 7): label AI content visibly, avoid realistic fabricated acts by real people, aim at policies, and act on takedown requests promptly. If you're targeted: save URLs and screenshots, report under the IT Rules, send a legal notice and seek urgent interim relief.

The same rules apply whichever party is in power.
