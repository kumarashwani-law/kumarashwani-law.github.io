AI training and copyright: three courts, three answers

Ashwani KumarAdvocate · 16 November 2026 · 3 min read
View on LinkedIn

In ten days, courts in Europe, Canada and the US each moved on the same question: can an AI company train on copyrighted work without permission?

🇪🇺 European Union (3 November)
In Like Company v. Google (C-250/25), Advocate General Szpunar said training is "as a rule, freely permitted" under the text-and-data-mining exception, if developers:
• use only lawfully accessible sources
• respect machine-readable opt-outs such as robots.txt
• take steps to stop the model reproducing specific works
But a chatbot fetching and reusing news in real time may still infringe press publishers' rights.

🇨🇦 Canada (13 November)
The Ontario Court of Appeal unanimously held that OpenAI must defend the Canadian news publishers' suit in Ontario. OpenAI argued that its training happened outside the province. The Court found "a real and substantial connection". Canadian fair dealing, not US fair use, will apply, and there is no text-and-data-mining exception. This decides venue only.

🇺🇸 United States (pending)
In In re OpenAI Copyright Infringement Litigation, Judge Sidney Stein will decide fair use on cross-motions for summary judgment, without a trial. On 6 November he allowed 56 amicus briefs.

🇮🇳 And India?
In ANI v. OpenAI, the Delhi High Court (24 July) kept jurisdiction, held training-related storage prima facie covered by Section 52(1)(a)(i), and refused an interim injunction. ANI's appeal is pending. Indonesia is legislating instead, and Google, Meta and TikTok opposed its bill on 11 November.

Three patterns across the courts:
1️⃣ Location isn't a shield. Delhi and Ontario both rejected "we trained abroad".
2️⃣ Opt-outs matter. The EU makes them the line, and the Delhi HC weighed the unused crawler opt-out against ANI.
3️⃣ Outputs are the weak point. Regurgitation is where liability sits.

My take: India shouldn't leave this to one interim order. Stretching "private use, including research" to cover commercial AI training is a fragile base for a whole industry. A clear statutory rule with opt-outs, transparency and anti-memorisation duties would serve creators and developers better.
