"Seeing is believing" may no longer work in court.

Ashwani KumarAdvocate · 24 October 2026
View on LinkedIn

On 15 October, OpenAI and Meta told the US Judicial Conference's Advisory Committee on Evidence Rules something remarkable:
🔹 OpenAI's policy counsel said she doesn't expect a scientific method to detect AI images in future, and that watermarking and verification "will never be completely reliable"
🔹 Meta said media from recent models "cannot be distinguished by humans from authentic content", and costs little to make

The US is drafting rules in response (neither is final):
1️⃣ Draft Rule 901(c): if a challenger shows enough evidence of AI fabrication (a bare allegation isn't enough), the party relying on the item must prove it is more likely than not authentic
2️⃣ Draft Rule 707: AI-generated evidence offered without an expert must meet expert-level reliability standards

Courts face two dangers: fake evidence getting in, and real evidence being dismissed by someone simply crying "deepfake" (the "liar's dividend").

Where India stands:
Section 63 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam requires a certificate with the record's hash value (Part A) and an expert's certification (Part B). The Supreme Court upheld this in May 2026. But a hash proves a file hasn't changed since it was hashed, not that it was real to begin with. A deepfake hashed at creation passes perfectly. And we have no specific rule on who must prove what once fabrication is alleged.

My take: India already has building blocks:
✔ A threshold test like Rule 901(c), so specific red flags shift the burden
✔ The February 2026 IT Rules already require AI tools to embed identifying metadata, which courts can ask for
✔ The Trade Marks Registry's draft AI rules already let it demand native files and metadata; courts can do the same
✔ More notified Examiners of Electronic Evidence under Section 79A of the IT Act

Practical checklist (slide 7):
If you rely on digital evidence: preserve the native file and device, record hash and metadata at collection, keep a chain-of-custody log, file the Section 63 certificate early, and corroborate.
If you challenge it: object early, point to specific red flags (no native file, missing metadata, odd timestamps), seek the original device and a forensic examination. Bare "it's AI" claims won't do.

Authenticity is the next big evidence fight. Indian courts should get ahead of it.
